## ABSTR ACT

```
Binocular rivalry (BR) occurs when each eye is presented with mutually incompatible images, and the brain alternates between
perceiving one image, the other or occasionally a mashup of both. Addressing a decades- old suggestion, it has been shown that
the competition between alternative representations in BR induces a pattern of neural activation resembling that occurring in
cognitive conflict, eventually leading to fluctuations between different perceptual outcomes in the case of steep competition. This
is reflected by known signatures of conflict dynamics, namely, an increase in fronto- medial theta oscillatory power (5–7 Hz) in
the EEG right before perceptual transitions and a decrease thereafter, as well as a decrease in parieto- occipital alpha oscillatory
power (8–12 Hz) prior to perceptual transitions and an increase thereafter. However, according to a growing body of research,
frontal activity during BR might be related to report processes rather than perception processing per se. Such conflation is related
to the use of continuous report protocols. To circumvent this confound, here, we present a BR study using an onset rivalry (rather
than continuous rivalry) protocol that dissociates the moment of report from the period of stimulus presentation. The findings
revealed higher fronto- medial theta power for rivalrous than for non- rivalrous stimuli both resulting in equivalent perceptual
classification, despite the absence of a motor confound. In addition, we found greater parieto- occipital alpha suppression for ri-
valrous stimuli. The results presented here advance our understanding of how cognitive conflict monitoring and resolution may
influence perception in the event of competition from incompatible sensory patterns.
```

In an earlier study (Drew et al.  2022 ), we argued that BR epit-

flict mechanisms at the neural level. This idea was not new;
it had first been articulated in the seminal formulation of the
conf lict monitoring and cognitive control theory (verbatim
from Botvinick et  al.  2001 , 464: ‘If the ACC does respond to
stimulus conflicts, one might expect this area to become ac-

According to the theory of conflict monitoring and cognitive
control, fronto- medial (fm) brain structures such as the ante-
rior cingulate cortex (ACC) are responsible for the monitoring
and detection of conflicts during information processing: If
incompatible representations are simultaneously activated,
then adjustments are triggered, putatively invoking areas in
the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) to recruit the rel-
evant resources to adapt and respond to the conflict, mini-
mising any consequent disruption and future reoccurrence.
Although the exact computations of the ACC have been under
discussion since the initial formulation of the theory (Vassena
et  al. 2 017), this frontal network remains essential for detec-
tion of disparities and control (Safavi and Dayan  2022 ). The
dynamics between conflict and control ensure efficient use
of limited resources. Evidence from electroencephalogra-
phy (EEG) and modelling suggests that conflict detection is
reflected by increases in oscillatory power within the theta
band (5–7 Hz) over frontal electrodes, originating in the ACC
(Cohen  2014 ). A lot of the empirical evidence for conflict pro-
cesses comes from classic sensorimotor protocols inducing in-
compatible response tendencies (e.g., Stroop or Flanker tasks)
(Cavanagh and Frank  2014 ; Cohen and Ridderinkhof  2013 ;
Pastötter et  al.  2013 ). However, despite the long- conjectured
involvement of conflict mechanisms in BR, it had not been
explicitly tested within the framework of conflict monitoring
and cognitive control until recent years (Drew et al.  2022 ; Roy
et al. 2 017).

Drew et  al.  ( 2022 ) set out to test this prediction directly, com-
bining a continuous rivalry paradigm with EEG. As predicted,
the results showed that the power of fm theta oscillations
(5–7 Hz) selectively increased right before perceptual alterna-
tions and decreased thereafter. This pattern suggested that the
conflict monitoring system is engaged during perceptual com-
petition in BR, its responses being strongest as the competition
between alternative percepts mounts (right before a switch in
perceptual dominance). In accordance with the theory, we also
investigated the consequences of conflict resolution via atten-
tional engagement and found that the power of parieto- occipital
(po) alpha oscillations (8–12 Hz) displayed an inverse pattern to
that of fm theta. In this case, po alpha reflected periods of high

```

change. Our results thus provided evidence for increased fronto-
medial activity during rivalry dynamics, adding to existing evi-
dence supporting the involvement of the ACC in BR (see Lumer
et al.  1998 and Sterzer et al.  2009 for an example from f MRI and
a review respectively or more recently Gelbard- Sagiv et al.  2018
for an example from single- cell recordings). In another study,
Weilnhammer et al. ( 2021 ) proposed that competition between
alternative representations of ambiguous stimuli engaged the
inferior frontal cortex (a region with functional connection to
the ACC; Monosov and Rushworth  2022 ), as decisive in adjudi-
cating between the two perceptual interpretations, a proposal
that is also supported by invasive recordings in primate studies
(Dwarakanath et  al.  2023 ). Building on early imaging studies
that proposed that prefrontal areas may play a role in driving or
regulating perceptual alternations through top- down control of
sensory processing, we attributed these results to dynamics of
conf lict monitoring and subsequent adjustments in control.
```

```
While these initial results were an encouraging step towards ad-
dressing a decades- old puzzle, they were not however without
limitations. One concern in particular was the presence of con-
tinuous motor report in Drew, Torralba et al.'s paradigm, which
is by far the most widely used approach to study rivalry dynam-
ics. In recent years, however, a growing body of research has
called into question the involvement of prefrontal areas in per-
ceptual multistability, with suggestions that these areas may re-
flect ancillary processes such as introspection, decision- making
or motor planning related to reporting perceptual change rather
than the perceptual process itself. In one debate- sparking f MRI
study, Frassle et al. ( 2014 ) used a ‘no- report’ BR paradigm, where
perceptual reversals were decoded from a combination of pup-
illometry and optokinetic nystagmus (OKN). They found that
when participants were not tasked with overtly reporting per-
ceptual reversals, differential frontal activity was absent when
viewing conspicuous and inconspicuous stimuli. These results,
as well as others, prompted the suggestion that prefrontal activ-
ity may reflect processes pertaining to the task report (such as
introspection and action) rather than perceptual processes per
se (Block  2019 ; Brascamp, Blake, and Knapen  2015 ; Odegaard
et al. 2 017). Although the relevance of prefrontal areas in percep-
tual multistability has mainly been discussed in the context of
the neural correlates of consciousness (NCC) (Koch et al.  2016 ;
see Tsuchiya et  al.  2015 for an overview), these findings none-
theless challenge the interpretation of our results, since they
make indistinguishable predictions concerning the expected
neural activity. Studies using BR (or other multistable phenom-
ena) to probe either the neural basis of phenomenal experience
or the involvement of conflict- related mechanisms would both
predict switch- related activity in frontal areas. In the first case
though, such activation would be attributed to reporting the
switches while in the second, it would be attributed to conflict
processing and ensuing perceptual experience. While we made
numerous attempts at controlling for motor activity, and given
the low spatial resolution of EEG, we cannot decisively rule out
motor influence as a confound in our results, due to continuous
report in our task design.
```

## 1.1 | Hypotheses

The theoretical framework of this study along with the hypoth-
eses, the analysis pipeline and the experimental procedures
described below were set up a priori and pre- registered on the
Open Science Framework (https:// osf. io/ btwkd/ ). We hypothe-
sised that (1) if the fm theta increase during continuous BR was
due to perceptual conflict and not to the interference of motor
confounds, conflict processing in onset BR will be reflected by
increased power in fm theta oscillatory activity, and (2) follow-
ing the same logic, a higher theta power will imply a higher level
of conflict processing. Therefore, we predicted significantly
higher power in fm theta band activity in the conditions that in-
volve interocular conflict (incongruent stimuli) when compared
to the other conditions without interocular conf lict (congruent
stimuli).

The critical contrast of interest in all analyses involved stim-
uli that generated the same perceptual evaluation from the
subjects—in our study: incongruent stimulus with pure per-
cept versus congruent stimulus with pure percept. These two
stimulus conditions should in principle render the same (or
very similar) subjective perception arising from very different
endogenous states. The pure percept in the incongruent pure
condition emerges from a resolved perceptual conflict be-
tween two competing representations, whereas the pure per-
cept in the congruent condition does not in principle involve
the resolution of a conflict. Hence, we expected fm theta to be
higher in the former condition. We tested this with directional
(one- tailed) statistical tests. Given our stimuli, subjective ex-
perience, especially in the case of interocular rivalry, can be of
a pure continuous percept (the one of main interest by hypoth-
esis) or a mixed percept (when the dominant percept changes
midtrial, or when it is composed of a piecemeal percept). The
number of trials for the comparisons of interest cannot be an-
ticipated precisely, since they depend on subjective reports.
To maximise the number of trials available for this contrast,

```
we tried to minimise the number of piecemeal percepts in our
paradigm.
```
```
However, a proportion of trials were perceived as mixed. We
therefore performed further exploratory analyses on these
mixed percepts. In particular, we looked for differences between
trials leading to mixed percepts vs. those leading to pure - the
perceptual outcomes of the incongruent condition; in this case,
for both types of trials, the stimulus is physically the same, since
it presents interocular conflict but has different outcomes; in
one, the conflict is resolved in favour of one unique dominant
percept (incongruent pure), whereas in the other, the outcome
is mixed (incongruent mixed). We predicted that, within incon-
gruent trials, those leading to a mixed percept will correlate with
a higher fronto- medial theta power compared to those leading to
pure percepts. That is, we assume that mixed percepts reflect a
steeper competition. However, becasue this prediction does not
follow directly from our main hypothesis, we tested for potential
differences between these two conditions using two- tailed tests.
```
```
As for the time windows of interest, we were initially interested
in the whole window of stimulus presentation, minus the first
500 ms following stimulus onset. However, we also planned an
exploratory analysis separating the window of stimulus presen-
tation into three 500- ms windows (0–500, 500–1000 and 1000–
1500 ms following stimulus onset). This enabled us to study the
temporal evolution of fm theta adjustments, which are also doc-
umented in close vicinity to the stimulus (as well as extending
beyond it), as well as throughout various stages of stimulus pre-
sentation (Cavanagh and Frank  2014 ; Drew et  al.  2022 ; Morís
Fernández et al. 2018).
```

## 2.2 | Apparatus and Stimuli

Participants were presented with two static circular rival Gabor
gratings (11.5° diameter; 0.1 contrast and 0.73 cycles/cm of spa-
tial frequency) of different orientation (±45°) and colour (green
and red), framed within a circle and with a fixation cross in the
middle (Figure 1C). Images were presented on a 19.8- in. CRT
monitor (1024 × 768; 120- Hz refresh rate) with a grey background
(10.7 cd/m^2 ) displayed at 80 cm from the participants' eyes. Each
image was presented to a different eye by means of stereoscope
mirror glasses (Figure 1B). Visual stimuli were created using
the Matlab Psychophysics Toolbox (RRID: SCR_002881) and
Matlab version R2017b (9.3.0.713579).

The green RGB value was matched to the subjective lumi-
nance of the red Gabor using an up/down procedure adapted
from Cavanagh and colleagues (Cavanagh et al.  1987 ) to mi-
nimise the flickering between red and green RGB Gabors at
a rate of 60 Hz. To achieve this, participants were instructed
to regulate the flickering until it had stopped completely, in-
dicating that the colours had been matched. This served to
prevent potential dominance of one percept based on stimulus
features (Levelt 1965).

experimental room for 5 min. The stereoscope mirrors were

## 2.4 | EEG Recording

During the experiment, EEG data were acquired using a
64- electrode system (actiCAP, Brain Products GmbH, Munich,
Germany) placed in accordance with the 10–10 international
system. The ground electrode was placed on AFz and the
online reference on the right mastoid. Electrodes for offline
re- reference were placed on the right and left mastoids. The
vertical electrooculogram was recorded by an electrode un-
derneath the right eye and the horizontal electrooculogram at
the outer canthus of the right eye. Impedance was kept below
10 kΩ for all electrodes. Signal was recorded via BrainVision
Recorder (Brain Products GmbH, Munich, Germany) at a sam-
pling rate of 500 Hz, connected to stimulus presentation via
parallel port.

## 2.5 | EEG Pre- Processing

Pre- processing and analyses of EEG data were done using
Fieldtrip (RRID:SCR_004849) and custom- made code in
Matlab. The pre- registered EEG analysis focused on the last
second of the stimulus presentation period (500–1500 ms fol-
lowing stimulus onset). Data corresponding to these periods
(hereby termed trials) were segmented for analysis. Trials
were classified into three conditions by stimulus type and re-
ported percept: congruent pure (congruent stimuli perceived
as pure red or green stimuli), incongruent pure (incongruent
stimuli perceived as pure red or green stimuli) and incongru-
ent mixed (incongruent stimuli perceived as piecemeal per-
cept). Data were inspected for artefacts in order to manually
reject trials contaminated by blinks, movements and noise
upon visual inspection (a widely employed method in the field
on account of the easily identifiable morphological signatures
[shape and duration] of blinks and other ocular movements,
allowing reliable identification by visual inspection; Zhang
et al.  2024 ). This resulted in 264 congruent trials and 396 in-
congruent trials. Participants with fewer than 30 trials in at
least one of the three conditions (after preprocessing) were
excluded from analysis, according to the pre- registered exclu-
sion criteria. Twenty- one subjects were rejected from analysis
before reaching our final sample of 47 participants meeting
inclusion criteria.

## 2.9 | Time- Resolved Analyses

An analysis was performed to examine the time course of theta
activity throughout the whole trial (−740 to 1500 ms). We calcu-
lated oscillatory power for each participant and trial type using
500 ms sliding windows in steps of 20 ms and 1 Hz in the cor-
responding ROI for the theta band (5–7 Hz) with a short- time
Fourier transform. A Hanning taper was applied to reduce spec-
tral leakage. Power was baseline- corrected by power calculated
in the −740 to − 240 ms time window (see above). Data were then
averaged over frequency, electrode and trial to obtain a single
timeseries per participant and condition. Grand averages were
then obtained across all participants for each condition. We
performed a Monte Carlo cluster correction for multiple com-
parisons (Maris and Oostenveld  2007 ) (alpha threshold = 0.05,
number of iterations = 10,000). We first compared the congruent
condition to the incongruent pure condition and then the in-
congruent pure condition to the incongruent mixed condition.
It should be noted that the aim of the current analysis is not to
provide reliable estimations of the onset/offset of the effect, only
to illustrate the timecourse of activity. In order to provide re-
liable estimation of onset and offset, other methods should be
used (Rousselet  2025 ).

## 2.10 | Time- Frequency Maps

Time resolved power was calculated for each subject, electrode
and condition separately by means of short- time Fourier trans-

from 2 to 20 Hz (in steps of 1 Hz) at times from −740 to 1500 ms

## 2.12 | Exploratory Analysis: Po Alpha Suppression

## (Time Course and Source Localisation)

```
A central aspect of our hypotheses and predictions from Drew
et al. ( 2022 ) concerned the role of alpha oscillations. According
to the conf lict monitoring hypothesis, one of the functions of
the ACC is to trigger strategic adjustments (i.e., attention ori-
enting) via other brain structures (e.g., the dlPFC) for conflict
```

## 4 | Discussion

Previous findings by Drew et al. ( 2022 ) substantiated the long-
conjectured hypothesis that BR engages a midfrontal neural
mechanism responsible for conf lict monitoring and cognitive
control across various levels of information processing, includ-
ing perception (Botvinick et al.  2001 ; Carter and Van Veen  2007 ;
Clairis and Lopez- Persem  2023 ). Drew et  al. ( 2022 ) found that
the power of fm theta oscillations (5–7 Hz), an established EEG
marker of conflict, selectively increases right before perceptual

```
alternations and decreases thereafter, suggesting that some
form of conflict monitoring occurs during perceptual compe-
tition in BR. One limitation to that previous approach was the
reliance on continuous motor report (via keypress) during stim-
ulus presentation, and thus periods of analysis of neural data.
This prevented us from fully excluding potential contamination
from motor processes (decision- making, preparation and execu-
tion), particularly in light of recent evidence suggesting frontal
activity during rivalry may primarily reflect these motor pro-
cesses, rather than the perceptual processes themselves. Here,
we presented participants ( N = 47) with a BR paradigm that sep-
arates in time the motor report from the stimuli, thus removing
motor contamination during periods of stimulus presentation,
using onset rivalry (Britz et al.  2011 ). We measured changes in
fm theta power to address the hypothesis that if conflict mech-
anisms are involved in BR, then we should expect theta power
increases in the absence of concurrent processes due to task
report.
```
```
The initially planned contrasts found no evidence of differential
fm theta power across congruent (no conflict) and incongruent
(conflict) conditions with equivalent perceptual report. However,
subsequent exploratory baseline- corrected power analyses look-
ing at a finer grained temporal scale revealed a different pattern.
We found that theta power during incongruent stimulus with
pure percept was higher than for the congruent condition (with
the same percept) during the central (500–1000 ms) time win-
dow, although the result became a trend after multiple compar-
ison correction and was not robust to hidden biases (Supporting
Information: 2. Sensitivity analysis), as well as when looking at
a time- resolved course of theta oscillations. These results are
weaker than predicted, but in line with the expected hypothesis.
```
```
When extending the exploratory analysis to the mixed percepts
trials, we found higher theta power in the incongruent pure
compared to the incongruent mixed conditions (in two time
windows: 500–1000 and 1000–1500 ms). This result goes against
```

However, predicting levels of conflict across incongruent mixed
and incongruent pure conditions remains a point of speculation.
On the one hand, incongruent mixed percepts might seem to be
the epitome of conflict since neither of the competing percepts
emerge to be fully dominant; hence, there is no clear winner in
the competition. On the other hand, while incongruent mixed
percepts present a more accurate reflection of the stimuli (in fea-
turing elements of each), incongruent pure percepts present a
conflict between the stimuli and the perceived percept (as only
one prevails in conscious awareness). While we had initially
opted for the first interpretation (higher conflict for the incon-
gruent mixed condition), the results align with a partial resolu-
tion of conflict in mixed percepts resulting in decreased power
compared to pure percepts. This could be explained by mixed
percepts including both trials including a transition between
percepts and piecemeal percepts. The size of our stimuli (11.5°)
was larger than the size proposed for rivalry in central areas (1°)
(Blake et al.  1992 ). Therefore, a portion of the piecemeal percepts
could be composed of local rivalry areas in which competition
resulted in a local pure percept. Therefore, we speculate that
conflict- related theta activity in piecemeal trials was equivalent
to the activity in incongruent pure trials. The decrease could be
explained by trials including a transition, in which theta activity
would increase prior to the transition but would decrease just
after it (Drew et al. 2022), explaining the lower theta power ob-
served in incongruent mixed percepts compared to incongruent
pure percepts. Other relevant findings include the behavioural
response times to stimuli, which were significantly faster in the
congruent condition, in line with known slowing down of re-
sponse times to conflicting stimuli due to interference (Wendt
et al.  2014 ). Exploratory analyses in po alpha (in time and at the
source level) also presented a clear pattern. Suppression was
greater for incongruent stimuli, consistent with the theory that
strategic adjustments are invoked following the detection of con-
flict, via functional inhibition of irrelevant information across
the sensory cortices.

Overall, the present study revealed weak but nonetheless con-
sistent modulation of conflict- relevant areas to rivalrous versus
non- rivalrous stimuli which are otherwise reported equally
by participants. While the magnitude of these effects remains
small, their presence is significant as a piece of counter evidence
to studies in which frontal activity had virtually disappeared in
the absence of report (Brascamp, Klink, and Levelt  2015 ; Frassle
et al.  2014 ; Tsuchiya et al.  2015 ). We hope that such results can
serve as a step towards further consolidating the role of frontal
areas in BR, and more specifically the involvement of conflict
mechanisms in perceptual processing more widely.

As described above, the paradigm used in this manuscript
represents a step forward from continuous report protocols in
separating motor report from stimulus presentation, as well as
associated processes such as motor preparation since the keys
for response options were randomised on each trial. It should
be noted that the ongoing debate surrounding the role of fron-
tal areas in perception does not invalidate the conflict hypoth-
esis we propose here. Frontal activity has been notably absent
in paradigms requiring no report from participants, prompt-
ing the suggestion that frontal areas may reflect introspection

```
and action tied to report rather than perception itself (Frassle
et  al.  2014 ). BR has been framed in predictive coding terms
(Hohwy et al.  2008 ; Weilnhammer et al. 2 017); when the physi-
cal features of both stimuli are matched, they have equal likeli-
hood based on sensory evidence and prior expectations: there is
no favourite in the competition; the dominant percept reinforces
a prediction that it will stay dominant, but the suppressed per-
cept, a persisting error signal, will eventually trigger a change in
dominance. We have argued elsewhere that the theory of con-
flict monitoring and cognitive control fits in well within such
accounts that view perception as a fundamentally active process
(Drew et al.  2022 ; Drew and Soto- Faraco  2024 ), likening conflict
signals to prediction errors. Predictive coding models suggest
that perceptual errors are continuously updated and resolved
through iterative processes between sensory and higher- level
brain regions. While frontal regions may register prediction er-
rors and modulate sensory processing, their involvement may be
less pronounced when perceptual states are less distinguishable
or behaviourally irrelevant. This means that even in the absence
of explicit reports, conflict monitoring and resolution can still
occur at a local level, with the broader network of regions con-
tributing when perceptual experiences or transitions are more
salient or behaviourally significant (see Brascamp et al.  2018 for
an in- depth discussion of this point).
```
```
Admittedly, proponents of ‘no post- perceptual cognition’ para-
digms might argue that the present findings are still limited since
the paradigm does not remove perceptual decision- making (par-
ticipants still must decide what they will report) nor awareness
of the perceptual change (which can happen even during brief
onset rivalry trials) (Block  2019 ). In the present study, we were
primarily interested in eradicating contamination from motor
processes, but did not attempt to eradicate ‘post- perceptual
cognition’ surrounding our stimuli. First, the theoretical con-
text of the conflict- monitoring process (unlike the search for
neural correlates of consciousness, NCC) inherently involves
higher- order cognitive functions such as perceptual decision-
making, attention and response selection. In the seminal the-
ory, conflict and cognitive control go hand in hand (Botvinick
et al.  2001 ), with conflict being a signal that increases attention
(i.e., cognitive control) to make sense of a given situation (note
that this could happen above or below thresholds of conscious
processing). Therefore, elements of ‘post- perceptual cognition’
are integral to understanding the role of conflict resolution
mechanisms throughout the information processing hierar-
chy. Post- perceptual processes like reporting, motor planning
or awareness are essential to the theory's premise because they
reflect how conflicts are detected, monitored and eventually re-
solved. This is not the case for NCC research, which seeks to
identify the minimal neural activity giving rise to conscious ex-
perience, independently from higher order cognitive processes.
Second, we cannot help but question the ecological validity of
stripping perceptual experience from all that surrounds it (from
perceptual decision- making processes to report), since evidence
shows these to be intricately linked processes for perception ‘in
the wild’ (for instance, making a perceptual decision and report-
ing it; Lepora and Pezzulo  2015 ; Ozbagci et al.  2021 ). We believe
disentangling report from stimulus presentation (even without
removing other associated cognitive processes) is a sufficient
step to inform the question of the involvement of conflict mech-
anisms in BR. What exactly each paradigm can contribute to
```

research in perceptual awareness is an open question and ongo-
ing discussion (Block 2019; Phillips and Morales 2020).

One potential limitation of the present study, as well as of other
similar studies, was the confound introduced by task demands.
Even with frequent breaks, BR experiments are strenuous, and
participants inevitably report fatigue due to sustained atten-
tion and repetitive reporting. Effort and cognitive exertion are
known to share neural correlates with those we discuss here:
an increase in mid- frontal theta power and decrease in poste-
rior alpha power, making cognitive load- related modulations
another possible confound in our results (Gevins et  al.  1998 ;
Pellouchoud et al.  1999 ). While, as noted by Drew et al. ( 2022 ),
such an explanation is not incompatible with the proposed
conflict account, it underscores the importance of finding al-
ternative, and less demanding, paradigms. We had hoped that
onset rivalry would present a lower- load alternative to ongoing
rivalry; however, participants still frequently reported compa-
rable levels of tiredness, suggesting that the onset paradigm,
while helpful, may only modestly reduce cognitive load. Future
work could address this by incorporating no- report paradigms,
shorter blocks or automated perceptual tracking to better isolate
neural processes of interest.

It is important to mention that the number of discarded subjects
in this study was very large (23). While some of them were dis-
carded during the artefact rejection (2), the vast majority did not
accomplish the behavioural criterion of 30 incongruent percepts
(20 subjects). A potential explanation for this large proportion
of subjects discarded can be interindividual variability in per-
ceptual duration across subjects. Our experiment was designed
to target a mean duration of 2500 ms, based on a previous study
(Drew et al. 2022 ), but percept duration is highly variable across
subjects (Kleinschmidt et  al. 2012) and has been correlated to
individual peak alpha frequency (Torralba Cuello et  al. 2022;
Katyal et al. 2019). It is possible that excluded subjects had faster
alpha rhythm and therefore shorter typical percept durations.

It has been suggested that both the onset rivalry paradigm used
here and the ongoing rivalry paradigm used in Drew et al. ( 2022 )
may engage frontal activity differentially. For instance, activity
in the DLPFC and ACC has been reported to be higher at the
onset of rivalry compared to the ongoing phase, particularly
when participants are explicitly reporting their perceptions; see
Lumer et al. ( 1998 ) and Sterzer et al. ( 2009 ). The results we pres-
ent here often show differences in fm theta beyond the stimulus
onset time rather than at the very onset, which suggests that this
may not be such a concern. Future studies explicitly comparing
activity in onset and ongoing paradigms could help to disentan-
gle this further.

Within perception research, BR has been central to discussions
on theories built around the notion of perception as inference. On
one hand, as we discussed earlier, BR has been framed according
to predictive coding terms (Hohwy et  al.  2008 ; Weilnhammer
et al. 2 017). On the other hand, it has been suggested to reflect
internal foraging, the outcome of which is influenced by inter-
nal and external values (Safavi and Dayan  2022 ). Varying stimu-
lus features of the rivalling inputs allows great liberty and scope
to test which sources of information help to determine domi-
nance (perceptual selection) of one stimulus over the other. For

```
instance, it has been shown that dominance is greater for more
salient stimuli (Brascamp, Klink, and Levelt  2015 ; Levelt  1965 ),
as well as emotional stimuli (Alpers and Gerdes  2007 ), aesthetic
stimuli (Mo et  al.  2016 ), expected stimuli (Leopold et  al.  2002 )
and stimuli that are congruent with other concurrent stimuli
in other modalities (Lunghi et al.  2010 ), just to name a few. As
mentioned above, we have argued elsewhere that the theory of
conflict monitoring and cognitive control fits in well within such
accounts that view perception as a fundamentally active process
(Drew et al.  2022 ; Drew and Soto- Faraco  2024 ; Ruzzoli and Soto-
Faraco  2025 ), likening conflict signals to prediction errors and
suggesting that switch- related activity may be explained as the
rising prediction error that culminates in the suppressed percept
becoming the dominant one. In some of the clearest empirical
support for this idea, f MRI data from Weilnhammer et al. (2 017)
estimated the time course of prediction errors during mul-
tistable motion perception using a Bayesian predictive- coding
model. They later argued that the inferior frontal cortex (IFC)
(a region with functional connection to the ACC) is a main
player in adjudicating between alternative representations of
ambiguous stimuli (Weilnhammer et al.  2021 ). Concerning the
ACC, a notable study by Gelbard- Sagiv et  al.  ( 2018 ) provided
compelling evidence showing that ACC activity precedes per-
ceptual switches by up to 2 s, with a marked increase in firing
rate before the switch and peak discriminability around or just
before the switch onset. This anticipatory response suggests that
the ACC plays a key role in initiating perceptual transitions.
Furthermore, the ACC's neural response profile during these
switches closely mirrors its activity in foraging tasks (Hayden
et al.  2011 ), linking perceptual switches to saccade- based forag-
ing behaviours. Taken together, the evidence points towards a
distributed network of regions, including the IFC and ACC, that
collaboratively support the processes of prediction and conflict
resolution during perceptual multistability and, more particu-
larly, binocular rivalry. These areas likely interact to monitor
and resolve sensory discrepancies, with their roles (and magni-
tude) dynamically adapting to the nature of the perceptual con-
flict. Our findings contribute to this framework, advancing our
understanding of how these regions work together to orchestrate
perceptual transitions and highlighting the importance of inte-
grating predictive and conflict monitoring processes in future
models.

```

```
understanding of how cognitive conf lict monitoring and reso-

will encourage discussion around how conflict processes fit in
with dominant frameworks of perception. We are also hopeful
that these will encourage future studies consolidating their role
across various levels of information processing and in other per-
ceptual phenomena more specifically.
```
